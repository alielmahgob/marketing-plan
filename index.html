Part I · Dealing with Disruptions

When the Plan Meets Reality

Half the resources. New demands. One sprint to defend.

Two Things Happened

The Bad News
Our partner team in India hit a major impediment.
  • We have half the capacity we planned for.
  • Across all sizes (S, M, L), we have to cut by 50%.
  • The disruption hit the backlog, not the team’s commitment.
What this means for us
We have to choose what to drop, and prove that what stays still works.
The Opportunity
The city wants to advertise Wasl publicly.
  • Key features must work perfectly.
  • Data security is not negotiable.
  • Ease of use is a must.
What this means for us
The city’s voice is a sharper compass than MoSCoW alone.
“Less capacity. Higher standards. Same promise.”

Three Decisions Before We Cut a Single Story

01
We listened to the city.
Their demands aren’t pressure. They’re prioritization signal. If “data security” is a blocker for them, it’s a Must for us, even though it wasn’t loud on our original backlog.
Outcome
City demands → new prioritization inputs.
02
We raised the bar, not the count.
Instead of adding new stories under pressure, we baked the city’s must-haves (data security, ease of use) into the Definition of Done for every Sprint 1 story. Same 15 stories. Higher acceptance bar.
Outcome
Story count stays 15. The bar each story must clear rises.
03
We changed the tool.
MoSCoW got us to Sprint 1. Under capacity pressure, we needed a sharper instrument: the Reinertsen Matrix.
Outcome
Importance → importance × cost of delay.
“We didn’t cut first. We re-decided first.”

Cutting the Slice Would Have Broken the Promise

The slice is already minimal.
We’d already cut MoSCoW Won’t-Haves before this disruption hit. There is no fat left in Sprint 1.
If we cut more
We start cutting muscle, not fat. The slice stops being minimal viable.
The slice is vertical, not horizontal.
Drop Magnus’s dashboard and Astrid’s check-in goes nowhere. Drop Lars’s fallback and the safety net disappears. Each story holds the others up.
If we cut one
The chain breaks. Astrid → Magnus → Lars only works end‑to‑end.
The city’s “key features” are exactly Sprint 1.
Daily check-in. SOS. Dashboard. Missed-check alert. Neighbor fallback. The city’s “perfect” features and our Sprint 1 are the same list.
If we cut here
There’s nothing left for the city to advertise. We lose the public launch.
We didn’t defend the sprint out of stubbornness. We defended it because cutting it means there’s no product left to advertise.

Reinertsen: The Tool the Crisis Demanded

Why MoSCoW wasn’t enough
Importance, but not trade-off under constraint.
  • MoSCoW had already pushed everything we could into Won’t-Haves.
  • It tells you importance, not trade-off under constraint.
  • “Must Have” is binary. It can’t help you choose between two Musts.
The gap
9 Musts on day one, 0 way to rank them.
What Reinertsen added
A sharper instrument for cutting under pressure.
  • Made delay cost explicit, not implicit.
  • Combined importance and effort in one view.
  • Cut along the right axis: low-payoff Larges go first, high-payoff Smalls stay.
The result
15 stories ranked 1–9 in a single afternoon.
“Different sprint, different problem, different tool. That’s how we got the answer.”

Three Things We Got Wrong, And What We Did About It

01
The mistake
We started with the prototype, not the vision.
Ali had a Flutter prototype before we wrote a vision statement. We were designing screens before we knew who they were for.
What we did
Stopped. Wrote the vision. Re-derived stories from personas.
The prototype became evidence, not the source.
02
The mistake
Cross-border standups were chaos at first.
Our partners in India joined dailies, but it was unclear which decisions were ours alone vs. theirs to weigh in on. Standups ran 35 minutes.
What we did
SM enforced the three-question format strictly.
Decisions and discussions moved to a separate sync. Standups now run 15 minutes flat.
03
The mistake
We almost confused MoSCoW with prioritization.
Early on, “Must / Should / Could / Won’t” felt like the answer. We had nine Musts.
What we did
Forced ourselves to use SML alongside MoSCoW.
Put real stories in Won’t-Have. When the disruption hit, Reinertsen taught us a third axis we’d been ignoring: cost of delay.

Three Lessons That Will Carry Forward

01
The vision is the cheapest thing to write and the most expensive thing to skip.
Once we wrote it, every prioritization conversation got 10× faster.
In practice
Write the vision in week 1. Re-read it before every prioritization debate.
02
Constraints don’t break a backlog. They reveal it.
Half the capacity made us decide what we actually meant by “Must.”
In practice
Don’t fear the cut. Treat constraints as the test your backlog needed.
03
No single prioritization tool is enough.
Story map, MoSCoW, SML, Reinertsen. Each answers a different question. Use the one the moment is asking for.
In practice
Match the tool to the question. Switch when the question changes.
